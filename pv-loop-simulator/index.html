About the model

How the simulation works

The left ventricle is a time-varying elastance: pressure moves between the end-diastolic curve, P = A·(eβ(V−V₀)−1), and the end-systolic line, P = Ees·(V−V₀), as an activation function rises and falls each beat. Relaxation speed stretches the fall of that activation; if relaxation is slow and the beat is short, it carries over into the next diastole.

The LV is coupled to a left atrium with its own passive and active elastance, mitral and aortic valves, an arterial Windkessel (characteristic impedance, compliance and SVR), a systemic venous reservoir, a pulmonary compartment, and a simple right-heart flow source that returns venous blood to the lungs in proportion to venous pressure (no right ventricle as such). Blood volume is conserved, so congestion and low output come out of the model rather than being imposed.

Valves follow the Bernoulli orifice relation, ΔP = (Q / (50·A))² with Q in mL/s and A in cm², so stenosis is set by valve area and regurgitation by effective regurgitant orifice area (EROA).

Limitations
  • It is a teaching model, not a patient-specific predictor. Numbers are plausible, not exact.
  • The right ventricle, pericardium, coronary flow and reflexes (baroreflex) are not modelled. Presets include typical compensations (heart rate, SVR, volume) set by hand, and they do not undo themselves when you treat the patient.
  • Volumes are in model units. The whole modelled stressed volume is about 590 mL, so the fluid and volume-loss buttons do not map one-to-one onto litres of real fluid.
  • HCM outflow obstruction is approximated as a fixed narrowing; real LVOT obstruction is dynamic.
  • Atrial fibrillation is shown as loss of atrial kick at a faster rate; beat-to-beat irregularity is not simulated.
